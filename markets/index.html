Market analyses

Cheap hours aren't rare. They're a resource.

Most thermal electrification evaluations assume a fixed price for electricity. Invariably, that price is too high to achieve significant thermal decarbonization, except where there is a large heat recovery opportunity.

We look at the other side of the coin: how to buy electricity when it's cheap and renewable. Our strategy is hybrid heating that keeps your existing boilers and adds an electric solution in parallel. When electricity prices are low enough, the heating moves to electric; when prices and carbon rise, it moves back to natural gas. The result is lower carbon, lower capex and lower opex.

At the heart of this is a simple fact: renewables push hourly electricity prices down, so when power is cheap, it is renewable. We analyze wholesale markets to see which technology options those cheap prices enable. Every number below is the last 12 months of hourly prices against delivered gas, for an electric boiler and a heat pump at COP 2.0 (two units of heat per unit of electricity), updated daily.

Utility rates

But the wholesale market isn't your utility bill.

We spend our time in utility tariff books, finding rates that reward flexibility with lower energy, demand, capacity, transmission and distribution charges. Ideally, we tap into real-time pricing programs that let you pick off only the cheapest and cleanest hours.

Wisconsin Public Service (MISO): real-time market pricing

On MISO's raw real-time price at the same zone, an electric boiler beat gas in 47% of hours, not 30%. The rate follows the day-ahead market, which misses many of the windy hours when real-time prices fall, and adds losses and a price floor.

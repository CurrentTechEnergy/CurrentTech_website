As grids add wind and solar, electricity prices drop in those highly renewable hours, resulting in electricity cheap enough to make heat for less than the cost of gas for thousands of hours a year. Keep your gas boilers. Add electric beside them.

An electric boiler runs in parallel with your existing natural gas boilers. It takes power only in cheap, highly-renewable hours, often power that would otherwise be curtailed, and the gas boilers carry the rest. Using electricity only during highly renewable hours results in low operating cost and low carbon emissions and the electric boiler minimizes equipment costs. The result is the lowest carbon, capex, and opex scope 1 decarbonization option.

Nothing downstream changes. The plant gets steam at the same pressure from the same header. When power prices spike the electric boiler is simply off and gas covers the load, so you take on none of the price risk that comes with going all-electric.

It also lines up with where carbon accounting is heading. The GHG Protocol's Scope 2 update may require matching electricity to the hour it was used, and a load that runs only in windy, sunny hours is the easy one to match. For the grid, it is demand that shows up exactly when wind and solar would otherwise be curtailed and stays off when the system is tight.

The hard part is the delivery charges.

Wholesale prices aren't what shows up on a bill. Almost no US utility offers truly cheap, intermittent electricity, and where hourly access to the wholesale market does exist, the distribution tariff usually undoes it.

For one client I evaluated an electric boiler to use negatively priced power in CAISO. PG&E's large commercial and industrial distribution tariff has a monthly non-coincident demand charge, billed on the site's highest draw no matter when it happens. For that boiler, the demand charge alone came to more than the cost of running on natural gas for the month, before buying a single kilowatt-hour.

But good rates exist, and they are worth finding. Wisconsin Public Service has a real-time market pricing rate that follows MISO's day-ahead price. On that rate, as billed, an electric boiler beat gas in 30% of hours over the last year, about 2,620 hours, and 50% of hours from April to June.

OG&E in Oklahoma has a Day-Ahead Pricing rate where only the load above your baseline pays the hourly price. A new electric boiler is all new load, so it runs entirely on that price, and your existing bill doesn't change. By our estimate from public SPP prices it beat gas in about 1,430 hours.

In ERCOT, a site connected at transmission voltage pays essentially the market price plus a small retailer fee. Its transmission charge is set by its draw during four summer peak intervals a year, and a boiler that stays off for those pays no transmission cost at all.

We have also had good results with municipal and co-op utilities that are willing to pass the actual costs incurred to a load.

So we spend most of our time in utility tariff books. We work with sites and utilities to identify tariff and contracting options that keep delivery charges as low as possible: real-time rates like these, rates whose demand charges follow coincident peaks you can stay off, and special contracts where neither exists.

For utilities and renewable owners

The perfect load: only shows up when it's cheap to serve.

An electric boiler at an industrial site can take power in exactly the hours you have surplus wind and solar, while avoiding triggering more transmission and capacity charges or build-out. That lets you sell more electricity through the system you already have.

What it does for you.

Dispatchable load absorbs excess renewables. More hours clear at $0/MWh or below every year, and someone holds the gap between zero and that $55 PPA on your books. A megawatt-hour that displaces gas in a boiler is worth about $15 in every hour it runs, so flexible heat puts a floor under those hours.

That means more electricity sold and higher utilization of your system. If you own or contract for renewable projects, it can also offset some of the losses that come with curtailment.

If you don't have renewable projects of your own, you can buy from the ISO or the imbalance market when prices are low and sell at a margin. Either way it is added revenue, and added revenue holds down rates for everyone else.

This load is essentially fully dispatchable. It only runs on what is left after you have served everything that pays more, so it never displaces higher-paying load. For the same reason it stays off the peaks and doesn't add to transmission, capacity or other peak-driven charges.

And every hour the electric boiler runs is an hour a gas boiler doesn't, which counts toward clean heat and gas decarbonization goals.

Two ways to structure it.

A non-firm, dispatchable tariff. Cheap, interruptible power in the hours you have surplus energy and headroom on the wires. You buy at wholesale, sell at a slight premium to flexible load, and keep the margin.

Heat as a service, sold as a hedge. CurrentTech Energy puts an electric boiler at an industrial site and sells the heat. The boiler buys power whenever the price is below about $15/MWh. That standing bid is a floor you can hold against your own PPAs or sell to offtakers.
